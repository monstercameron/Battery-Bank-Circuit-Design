Battery Bank / Travel RouterPack one box,
not five.

Travelling with a laptop usually means a power bank, a travel router, a flashlight and a Bluetooth speaker, plus a mesh radio if you go off-grid. This open design puts all five in one 150 × 94 × 70 mm aluminium case. Plug it into a hotel's Ethernet jack and you have your own Wi‑Fi 6 network. Its four USB‑C ports charge up to four laptops and phones at once, take a charger on any port, and give a laptop 2.5 Gb/s wired Ethernet over the same cable. The 90 Wh battery is under the 100 Wh airline limit, so it flies without airline approval.

Sheet 01

Why build this

Every gadget in a travel bag is one more thing to charge, one more cable and often one more charger. Putting them in one box only helps if they can all run at the same time and the box stays safe while they do. So the design starts from one hard case: everything switched on, from the battery alone, with every part working at no more than a third of its rating.

Everything at once

The 125 W battery budget covers all four USB‑C ports, USB‑A, DC out, the Qi pad, the router, Ethernet, audio and light together. Plug in a charger and its power goes to whatever is running first; only the surplus charges the pack.

Every port works both ways

Each USB‑C port has its own bidirectional power stage, so any port can charge a laptop or take a charger. Each one also carries 2.5 Gb/s Ethernet into a built-in switch and an OpenWrt router.

Safe with open firmware

The limits live in hardware: the cartridge BMS, fuses, resistor-set charger ceilings and an independent over-temperature cutoff. The firmware can be open and hackable because it can't push past them.

Four places it earns its space

Each scenario uses figures from the power budget. They are design limits, not measurements: nothing has been built yet.

Scenario A

The hotel room

The room has one Ethernet jack and patchy Wi‑Fi. Plug the jack into the RJ45 and the built-in OpenWrt router makes your own Wi‑Fi 6 network for everything you carry. A laptop on any USB‑C port gets power and 2.5 Gb/s wired Ethernet over one cable. Put the laptop's charger on another port and the bank runs on pass-through: with a 100 W charger, the other ports can still give up to 60 W each while the pack charges.

Plain wordsWhat is pass-through?+

Pass-through means using the bank while it is plugged in. Here every input and every load connects to the same battery node, VBAT. Power from a charger feeds whatever is running first, and only what is left over charges the cells.

Unplug the charger and the battery is already on that node, so the loads carry on from it. Try it above: switch on C1 in and watch the battery go from supplying power to receiving it.

Plain wordsWhy 125 W, and why 8.3 A but an 8.0 A limit?+

The Samsung 50S cell is rated for 25 A. A third of that is 8.3 A, and at the empty-pack voltage of 15 V, 8.3 A is 125 W. That is the guaranteed figure, because a fuller pack has a higher voltage and gives more watts for the same current.

The three numbers stack up like this:

  • 8.0 A: where firmware stops, a little under the 3:1 ceiling.
  • 8.3 A: the 3:1 ceiling the parts are sized for.
  • Above 8.3 A, below 25 A: where the BQ77915 protector in the cartridge cuts off in hardware, as a backstop.

Right at the bottom of the pack, 8.0 A is 120 W, so the allocator trims the USB‑C offers by a few watts there.

Plain wordsWhat are USB PD, EPR and PPS?+

USB Power Delivery (PD) is how a USB‑C charger and a laptop agree on power. They talk over the cable first, then the charger raises the voltage to what was agreed. The standard range tops out at 20 V × 5 A, which is 100 W.

EPR (Extended Power Range) adds higher voltages such as 28 V. This design supports 28 V but keeps each port at 100 W by limiting current to 3.57 A, which is what the 3:1 rule allows for the port's power stage.

PPS (Programmable Power Supply) lets a phone ask for its voltage in small steps while it charges, and the port follows. OnePlus phones fast-charge this way over USB‑C.

Plain wordsWhy Ethernet over USB‑C, and why 2.5G?+

Many laptops have no Ethernet jack any more. Here each USB‑C port has its own USB network adapter chip (RTL8156BG), so the same cable that charges the laptop also gives it a wired 2.5 Gb/s connection, with no dongle. The four adapters join an RTL8372N switch, which links to the Wi‑Fi 6 router and the RJ45.

2.5G won over 5G because the RTL8156BG is stocked at JLCPCB and costs less than the 1G chip, while the 5G RTL8157 isn't stocked and needs 10 Gb/s routing.

One honest caveat: the bank has to swap USB data roles with the device, and some phones may refuse. That is on the risk list to test.

Plain wordsWhy 90 Wh, in a swappable cartridge?+

Airlines let you carry lithium batteries under 100 Wh without asking for approval. Five Samsung 50S cells in series give 90 Wh, comfortably under that line, and every cartridge is labelled with its Wh rating. Always check your airline's rules before you fly.

The cells, BMS, fuse, temperature sensor and ID chip all live in a 112 × 82 × 25 mm cartridge that slides in from the right-hand end, so the battery's own protection always travels with the cells.

Plain wordsWhy two boards instead of one?+

Six 100 W-class power stages, each with a 17 × 17 mm inductor, fill most of one 124 × 86 mm board. So the power stages get their own board (A, with thicker 2 oz outer copper for current), and the networking, router module, radios and logic sit on board B above it, joined by a 2 × 20 stacking header.

Both boards, the case and the website's 3D model read one file, mech_spec.json, so they can't drift apart.

Sheet 06

Safety by design: 3:1

Every part that carries current or dissipates power is rated for at least three times its worst-case operating current or power, and junctions run at least 40 °C below their maximum. That is why the pack gives 125 W and not 187 W, and why the Qi pad is 5 W. Each row is drawn to its own scale: the full bar is the part's rating.

PartOperating vs rated (dashed line = ⅓)Ratio

    Cell row: 8.3 A is the 3:1 ceiling the parts are sized for. Firmware holds the pack to 8.0 A, and the cartridge's BQ77915 protector trips in hardware above 8.3 A but below the 25 A cell rating. Striped bars are the documented exceptions, explained under “Documented exceptions”.

    In plain words

    A 3:1 safety buffer means no part is ever asked for more than a third of what it is rated to do. A cell rated for 25 A is never asked for more than 8.3 A. An inductor rated for 22 A carries about 7 A.

    Parts working well inside their ratings run cooler and leave room for what a datasheet can't predict: a hot car, a blocked fan, a worn cable. The cost is power on paper: 125 W from the battery instead of 187 W, a 5 W Qi pad, a ~1.5 W flashlight. The design takes that trade on purpose.

    Voltage is different

    3:1 does not apply to voltage. Voltage uses standard derating against the worst-case net voltage.

    PartRuleExample
    Ceramic caps≥ 2×DC-bias loss
    MOSFET VDS≥ 1.5×VBAT 21 V → ≥ 40 V; VBUS 28 V → ≥ 60 V
    Diodes VR≥ 1.5×working voltage

    Documented exceptions

    • USB‑C contacts. USB PD defines 5 A; no 15 A USB‑C exists. 5 A receptacles, the negotiated contract is never exceeded, and an NTC at each connector folds back on heat.
    • USB‑A connector. No high-current USB‑A is stocked, so the 2 A part runs at 1.5 A.
    • PoE pairs. 802.3bt sets the per-pair current; magnetics are bt-rated.

    Enforced in hardware

    The cartridge BMS, fuses, resistor-set charger ceilings and an independent over-temperature cutoff hold the limits. Open firmware can't make the pack unsafe.

    Sheet 07

    Architecture

    Three planes share one board pair: the power path around VBAT, the 2.5G network, and the control and radio bus. Stacked boxes mean one per USB‑C port.

    Power path, in short

    Six BQ25756 buck-boost converters do the heavy lifting: one per USB‑C port in both directions, one forward-only for the DC jack and PoE, one reverse-only for DC out. They all meet at VBAT, where the cartridge sits.

    Network, in short

    Each USB‑C port is also a 2.5G network adapter. The four port adapters and the RJ45 all meet at one RTL8372N switch, and the Wi‑Fi 6 router module sits on that switch, so wired and wireless devices share one network.

    Control, in short

    The ESP32 is always on. It runs the app and the power allocator, and talks to each power stage on its own I²C branch, because every BQ25756 has the same fixed address. The LoRa node is a separate chip so it can relay with everything else off.

    Sheet 09

    How it's being built

    A battery that charges four laptops deserves more trust than “it worked on my bench”. So the process is built to be checked by someone else: every rule is written down, every part choice is a script you can re-run, every datasheet value has a citation, and every change is in a public dev log.

    1. 1

      Rules first

      The 3:1 buffer, voltage derating, test points and isolation jumpers are written into the design rules before parts are chosen, so the parts have to fit the rules and not the other way round.

    2. 2

      Parts picked by script from JLCPCB's catalogue

      jlc.py searches the public JLCPCB parts library. pick_passives.py picks every resistor and capacitor with voltage margin over its worst-case net, prefers Basic, then Preferred, then Extended parts, and requires enough stock for a small build.

    3. 3

      Datasheet values, cited

      38 datasheets and app notes are downloaded and indexed. Each value taken from one, such as a resistor ratio, an address strap or a capacitor minimum, is quoted in the code next to where it is used.

    4. 4

      Schematic generated from Python

      Thirteen block scripts write 16 KiCad sheets. Every connection is a line of code that can be reviewed, diffed and re-run, and nothing is hand-edited in the generated files.

    5. 5

      Automatic checks on every build

      A checker tests voltage derating, the 3:1 rule, single-pin nets and missing LCSC numbers, then KiCad's own ERC runs. The build fails on any error.

    6. 6

      Adversarial review, then repair, then review again

      Three reviewers try to break the design, one each for power and safety; high-speed and networking; and control, radios and DFM. Findings are logged by severity and fixed in the Python, never in the output. Layout waits until no critical or major finding is open. Review process

    Caught by the process so far

    Real corrections from the dev log, each made before any copper was ordered.

    PD config memory was too small

    The TPS26750 datasheet requires at least 36 KB. The planned 32 KB EEPROM became a 64 KB M24512.

    A buck that couldn't reach its voltage

    The NIC core converter's 70 ns minimum on-time rules out 21 V → 0.95 V at 500 kHz, so it now runs at 310 kHz.

    DC input cutoff lowered to the jack's rating

    26.1 V became 24.3 V, which also lets 50 V capacitors meet the 2× voltage rule.

    Four PHY chips that weren't needed

    The RTL8372N switch integrates its PHYs, so four RTL8221B chips came out of the plan.

    An antenna the case would detune

    The ESP32 module moved to the U.FL-antenna version because the aluminium case would detune a PCB antenna.

    Sheet 11

    Glossary

    The short words used on this page, in plain language.

    VBAT battery node
    The one power rail that the cartridge, every input stage and every load connect to. 15.0 V when the pack is empty, 21.0 V when full.
    3:1 safety buffer
    Every part that carries current or makes heat is rated for at least three times what it will ever do here. It applies to current, power and heat, not voltage.
    Pass-through
    Using the bank while it charges. Input power serves the running loads first; the surplus charges the cells.
    USB PD Power Delivery
    How USB‑C chargers and devices agree on voltage and current before power flows. Up to 20 V × 5 A, 100 W, in the standard range.
    EPR Extended Power Range
    The part of USB PD that adds higher voltages such as 28 V. Here 28 V runs at 3.57 A so each port stays at 100 W.
    PPS Programmable Power Supply
    A USB PD mode where the phone asks for its voltage in small steps. OnePlus phones fast-charge this way.
    PoE Power over Ethernet
    Power and network on one Ethernet cable. 802.3bt Type 3 gives about 51 W at the device; about 46 W remains after the isolated converter.
    BMS battery management system
    The protector in the cartridge (BQ77915) that disconnects the cells on over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current or over-temperature, plus a fuel gauge (BQ34Z100).
    DFM design for manufacturing
    Making sure the boards can actually be made and assembled: stocked parts, correct footprints, spacing and assembly side. One of the three reviewers covers it.
    JLCPCB
    The PCB fabrication and assembly service the boards are designed for. Its parts catalogue is searched by script, and parts are identified by LCSC numbers.
    Basic / Extended part
    JLCPCB's part classes. Each unique Extended part adds a loading fee, so the picker prefers Basic, then Preferred, then Extended.
    ERC electrical rules check
    KiCad's own check of the schematic for things like unconnected pins and mismatched symbols. It runs on every build alongside the project's design check.
    OpenWrt
    Open-source router firmware. It runs on the Wi‑Fi 6 router module inside the bank.
    Meshtastic / LoRa
    LoRa is a long-range, low-power radio. Meshtastic is open firmware that turns LoRa radios into an off-grid text-messaging mesh that phones join over Bluetooth.
    NIC / PHY
    A NIC is a network adapter (here one RTL8156BG per USB‑C port). A PHY is the chip that drives the actual Ethernet cable.
